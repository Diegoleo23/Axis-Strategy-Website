Why Good Marketing Starts With Listening

Written from years spent in customer service, sales, key account management, and marketing. On listening as the real starting point.

James Chikaonda
August 2026 · 6 min read
WhatsApp LinkedIn Facebook Email Copy Link

Many organisations believe good marketing starts with creating campaigns.

I believe it starts much earlier. I remember reading one of my first marketing textbooks from CIM, which simply described marketing as the discipline of understanding customer needs and then satisfying them. That line stood out to me, and it basically spells out one thing about good marketing. It starts with listening.

Most organisations treat marketing as something that begins when the brief arrives. A new product. A new promotion. A new advert. A new slogan. A new launch date pinned on a wall. By the time marketing teams are sitting in a room debating taglines, the most important work has often already been skipped. Have they actually understood what customers are looking for? Or are they simply doing business-as-usual for the organisation, or fulfilling an instruction handed down by executives?

Good marketing does not begin with speaking. It begins with listening.

Listening Is More Than Research

When most organisations talk about listening to customers, they reach for the same familiar tools. Surveys. Focus groups. Dashboards. Net promoter scores. These have their place, and I have used them throughout my career. But they are only a small part of what listening actually means.

Listening is also the customer complaint that comes through the call centre at seven in the morning.

It is the conversation a branch officer has with a frustrated client who has queued for forty minutes.

It is the comment under a social media post that an intern flagged, but no one followed up on.

It is the question a sales representative keeps hearing in the market and cannot get a clear answer to.

It is the customer who quietly closed their account and never came back, and whom no one bothered to call.

It is the competitor whose product is suddenly gaining traction for reasons your team has not stopped to understand.

It is the staff member who has been with the organisation for fifteen years and has watched three previous campaigns try and fail to solve the same problem.

The People Closest to the Customer

Throughout my career, I have worked in customer service, sales, key account management and marketing. Across different industries and very different organisations, one observation has followed me everywhere I have gone. In the FMCG companies I worked in, they called it "Living in the Market." It was the only way we knew what was happening and what was needed.

The people closest to the customer often understand the customer best. There are examples of this beyond FMCG. Silicon Valley is one of them, a space notoriously focused on product and technology that learned, during the 2010s, that building beautiful code was not enough. This gave rise to Steve Blank's now-famous instruction to founders: "Get out of the building." It is the same philosophy as living in the market, expressed in different language. And on the opposite side, the stories of Nokia and BlackBerry, companies that stopped listening in time, are cautionary tales worth their own articles.

Understanding all this, it is striking how rarely staff on the frontline are the first people organisations ask when building campaigns.

Marketing teams will spend weeks debating positioning amongst themselves in the department, while the answers they need are walking through the front door every day. The branch teller knows why customers are frustrated. The call centre agent knows which products confuse people. The sales representative knows which objections come up in every meeting. The customer service officer knows the exact phrase customers use when they are about to leave.

This knowledge does not arrive in a report. It does not always make it into a survey. It sits in conversations, observations and patterns that frontline staff carry with them every day, often without realising how valuable it is.

The organisations that listen well are the ones that treat this knowledge as an asset rather than as gossip.

Listening Creates Better Decisions

When organisations listen properly, marketing changes shape.

It stops being about ticking a box or convincing people. It starts being about understanding them.

Products are designed around real problems rather than assumed ones. Pricing is set with a clearer view of what customers actually value. Positioning becomes sharper because the language is borrowed from how customers already describe their lives. Communication becomes simpler because there is less guessing about what will land. Innovation becomes more honest because it begins with a need rather than an idea looking for a market.

I have sat in enough campaign reviews to know what happens when this step is skipped. The creative is beautiful. The media plan is sophisticated. The launch event is polished. And yet the results are quiet, not because the execution failed, but because the campaign was answering a question customers were not actually asking.

A marketing team that listens well will produce fewer campaigns, but stronger ones. A marketing team that does not listen will stay busy producing campaigns that look good in internal presentations and disappear in the market.

Listening Never Stops

This is where many organisations struggle.

Listening is not something you do before a launch. It is something you continue after one.

The market changes. Customer expectations shift. Competitors move. The economy turns. What was true about your customer six months ago may not be true today. A campaign that worked last year may not work next year, and the organisation that assumes its customer is the same one it studied two years ago is already behind.

Good marketers stay curious. They keep asking questions even when they think they know the answers. They go back to the branches. They sit in on call centre shifts. They read the complaints that did not make it into the monthly report. They follow up with the customer who left.

Great marketers never assume they have finished learning.

Closing Thought

Every organisation wants customers to listen to its message.

Perhaps the better question is whether the organisation has first taken the time to listen to its customers.

Customers do not expect organisations to know everything.

They expect organisations to care enough to listen.
